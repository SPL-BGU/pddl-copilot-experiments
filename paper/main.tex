\begin{abstract}
% TODO(Omer): quotes the earlier Claude runs (Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.6), now in the supplement; the new run is in Table~claude
Planners and validators are correct by construction, and language models can now
call them as tools. We ask three questions about that access: whether the tools help
a model on planning-related tasks, whether the model chooses the right tool, and
whether it uses what the tool returns. We evaluate five open-weight and two frontier models on five
PDDL tasks, with and without a suite of seven planning tools, and a deterministic
oracle grades the final answer each model delivers. The tools help on most measures.
With them the models deliver a valid plan in $78$--$95\%$ of trials. Unaided, the
three open-weight models solve $43$--$69\%$ of the problems under a corrected system
message, and the tools are ahead by $49$, $38$ and $9$ points, the last with an
interval that reaches zero. The two frontier models solve $22$--$29\%$ unaided and
$95\%$ with the tools. Verdict errors on plans fall for four of the five models measured. The benefit shrinks as problems grow, and state traces degrade with length
in every model. Models usually choose the right tool, but one calls the plan validator
in only $20\%$ of trials until a single sentence tells it to, and on plan generation
models often add calls they do not need. The tool's output is often lost when the
answer is long. Almost no verdict is lost, but $5$--$13\%$ of correct plans are, and
the open-weight models lose $70$--$91\%$ of correct state traces ($66$--$80\%$ when
% TODO(Omer): points at material moved to the supplement (paper/supplement_source.tex)
a second answer layout is also read). At our context
window the largest cause is a final turn with no room left, yet a pilot with a larger
window removes most of those empty answers and recovers only part of the loss. On
PlanBench, the same tools lift an obfuscated benchmark from $0$ to $72\%$. The study
grades $273{,}600$ open-weight trials over public and renamed domains, and a
pre-registered rerun of $47{,}040$ trials that stores every answer in full.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% DRAFT 2026-06-14 (goal 1, partial).
% Findings preview must match Results; no "first" claims.
Large language models are increasingly deployed as agents that call external
tools, and automated planning is a clean setting in which to ask whether that
helps. Decades of research have produced planners and validators that
are \emph{correct by construction}: a plan that a sound solver returns provably
reaches the goal, and a verdict that a sound validator issues is correct
\citep{helmert2006fastdownward, howey2004val}. A language model that can call such
a tool should be able to hand over the parts of a PDDL task on which language
models are known to be unreliable, namely search, state tracking and plan checking
\citep{valmeekam2023planbench, kambhampati2024llmmodulo}. We study whether this
helps, whether the model picks the right tool, and whether it then uses what the
tool returns.

These questions are not settled. One line of work tests LLMs as planners.
Earlier models fell well short of solvers \citep{valmeekam2023planbench}, and the
newest frontier reasoning models are reported to rival strong planners on freshly
generated tasks \citep{correa2025frontier}. Both results describe the model alone. A
second line uses LLMs as \emph{formalizers} that translate language into PDDL for a
downstream solver \citep{liu2023llmp, tantakoun2025survey}, where the solver is always
called. A third measures tool-use competence without a planning oracle
\citep{bfcl2025}. These lines leave open what happens when the same model is given
sound planning tools that it is free to call: whether its answers improve, which tool
it calls, and whether the tool's result reaches its final answer.

% TODO(Omer): quotes the earlier Claude runs (Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.6), now in the supplement; the new run is in Table~claude
We evaluate five PDDL tasks (\emph{solve}, \emph{validate\_domain},
\emph{validate\_problem}, \emph{validate\_plan}, \emph{simulate}) on five open-weight
models from the Qwen and Gemma families and on two frontier models, Claude Haiku~4.5
and Claude Sonnet~4.6. Each model answers every task without tools and with a suite of
seven planning tools offered through the Model Context Protocol. The open-weight
models are also run under a prompt with one added sentence that names the tool. A
deterministic oracle
grades the final answer the model delivers, and every proportion carries a confidence
interval. The main sweep holds $273{,}600$ open-weight trials over the public domains
and over a renamed copy that controls for memorized domains. That sweep stored only
the first $500$ characters of each answer, so a pre-registered rerun of $47{,}040$
trials with answers stored in full supports every comparison that needs the complete
answer. We organize the study around three research questions.

\emph{RQ1: do PDDL tools help an LLM do planning-related tasks?} On most measures they
do, and less as problems grow. With the tools the models deliver a valid plan in $78$--$95\%$ of
the trials. Without them the three open-weight models solve $43$--$69\%$ of the
planning problems and the two frontier models $22$--$29\%$. The open-weight baseline

comes from a control. The unaided system message of the original runs referred to a
schema that the models never received, so we ran that arm again with a message that
states the answer format, and we read both arms with a second reading of the answers
that we adopted after the first run and before the control. Against this baseline the tools are ahead by $49$ and $38$
points for two models, and for the third the difference is $9$ points with an interval
that reaches zero. Verdict errors on plans fall with the tools for both Qwen models and both
Claude models, and stay where they were for the one model that seldom calls the
validator. State traces are the hard case. Their accuracy falls with the length of the
trace in every model, and on this task the tools show no gain for any open-weight
model. A plan delivered
with tools is almost always the planner's plan, so its length is the planner's.

\emph{RQ2: does the LLM choose the right tool?} When a model calls a tool on a
validation task or on \emph{simulate}, it almost always calls the right one and no
other. Calling at all is less certain: one model calls the plan validator in $20\%$ of
the trials under the plain prompt and in $93\%$ once a single sentence tells it to. On
\emph{solve} the models call the fitting planner and then add calls, so only
$22$--$39\%$ of the open-weight trials under the plain prompt call it alone.

answer. Plans are lost in $5$--$13\%$ of the trials in which the planner returned a
valid one. State traces are lost in $70$--$91\%$ of such trials by the open-weight

Supporting analyses cover the token cost of the tools, reasoning mode, a control for
memorized domains, and an external benchmark, PlanBench. We make the following
contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item A controlled comparison of the same models with and without sound PDDL tools on
five tasks, graded at the delivered final answer, with four measures of benefit: verdict
errors, state accuracy against trace length, problems solved against hardness, and
plan length.
\item A classification of tool choice with every tool offered, which separates calling
the right tool, calling only the right tool, and calling other tools as well, under two
prompts and two reasoning modes.
\item A measurement of how often a correct tool result fails to reach the final
answer, with loss categories that were registered before the data were read.
\item A pre-registered rerun with full answer storage, the complete prompts, and
supporting analyses of cost, reasoning mode, memorization and an external benchmark.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
% DRAFT 2026-06-14 (goal 2). Re-derived for our framework.

Automated planning studies how to find a sequence of actions that turns an
initial state into one that satisfies a goal. The Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL)
\citep{mcdermott1998pddl, fox2003pddl21} is the field's standard formalism, and decades of
research have produced sound, complete solvers such as Fast Downward
\citep{helmert2006fastdownward} and FF \citep{hoffmann2001ff} for classical planning and
ENHSP \citep{scala2016enhsp} for numeric planning, together with plan validators such as
VAL \citep{howey2004val}. These systems are correct by construction. A plan they return
provably reaches the goal, and a verdict they issue is correct. They depend, however, on
the formal modelling step, since a domain or problem must be written in precise PDDL
before any solver can run. Where a model must reason in the solver's place, numeric
domains are harder, because tracking arithmetic effects on continuous fluents adds to the
discrete state tracking that language models already find difficult.

\subsection{LLMs as Planners and as Plan Checkers}
PlanBench \citep{valmeekam2023planbench} gives a model a planning problem in natural
language, asks it for a plan, and checks the plan with VAL. Prompted this way, even strong
LLMs fall well short of a classical planner. \citet{kambhampati2024llmmodulo} argue from
this evidence that autoregressive LLMs cannot plan or verify their own plans by
themselves, and propose LLM-Modulo frameworks, in which the model works in a loop with
sound external verifiers. Our study tests a simpler arrangement. The model may hand a
single task to a sound tool, and there is no generate-and-verify loop.

Reasoning models raised these scores, at first without closing the gap on larger or
obfuscated problems. On PlanBench's $600$ Blocksworld problems, o1-preview produced a correct plan for
$97.8\%$, but for $52.8\%$ of the obfuscated Mystery Blocksworld problems and $23.6\%$ of
$110$ larger Blocksworld problems \citep{valmeekam2024o1prelim}. The journal version of
that evaluation adds that the gains come with large and unpredictable inference costs, and
that o1 improves further inside a compound system with a sound verifier
\citep{valmeekam2025o1tmlr}. In a later evaluation, \citet{correa2025frontier} give
frontier LLMs the PDDL files of $360$ newly generated tasks from $8$ domains of the IPC
2023 learning track, with two solved examples in the prompt, one attempt per task and no
tools. They check each plan with VAL and report that frontier LLMs now rival
state-of-the-art planners, and they replace every name with a random string to control
for memorization.

Other benchmarks split planning into the judgments a planner makes along the way.
ACPBench \citep{kokel2025acpbench} generates boolean and multiple-choice questions from
PDDL for seven such tasks over $13$ domains, so every answer is provably correct. Two of
its tasks are close to ours. Progression asks how an action changes the state, which our
\emph{simulate} task asks for every step of a plan. Validation asks whether an action
sequence is a valid plan, as \emph{validate\_plan} does. With chain-of-thought prompting
and two examples, GPT-4o scored $52.5\%$ on the multiple-choice validation questions, its
lowest task. ACPBench Hard \citep{kokel2026acpbenchhard} asks open-ended versions of the
same tasks, scores each with a symbolic validator, and finds that with few exceptions
every tested model stays below $65\%$. In both benchmarks the model answers without tools.

A related line asks whether a model can check plans itself. With GPT-4 as both plan
generator and plan verifier on Blocksworld, the verifier accepted $38$ of $45$ invalid
plans, and the loop with this verifier solved $55$ of $100$ problems against $88$ with VAL
in its place \citep{valmeekam2023selfcritique}. \citet{stechly2025selfverification} extend
the analysis to three domains, STRIPS planning among them, and find that performance
collapses under self-critique and improves under sound external verification. Outside
planning, \citet{huang2024selfcorrect} report that LLMs struggle to correct their
reasoning without external feedback. Our \emph{validate\_plan} task asks the question
these studies ask, whether a given plan is valid. The difference is that the model may
pass the plan to a sound validator, and we record whether it does.

\subsection{LLMs as Planning Formalizers}
A second line uses the LLM as a formalizer. The model translates natural language into a
formal model, and a sound solver does the search. LLM+P \citep{liu2023llmp} pipelines an
LLM into a classical planner, and \citet{guan2023world} use LLMs to build world models for
model-based planning. Plansformer \citep{pallagani2022plansformer} fine-tunes a model for
plan generation, and \citet{silver2024generalized} apply pretrained LLMs to generalized
planning in PDDL domains. The survey by \citet{tantakoun2025survey} treats LLMs as tools
that write and refine planning models for off-the-shelf planners, and notes that
end-to-end planning by an LLM still lacks soundness guarantees.

\citet{huang2025limit} measure the limits of this approach with zero-shot prompting and no
tools. Models are largely robust to lexical perturbation of domain symbols, and
formalization degrades as the descriptions become more natural. On their obfuscated
Mystery Blocksworld, GPT-4o as a formalizer produced $70$ of $100$ correct plans against
$0$ of $100$ as a planner, graded with VAL. The advantage is not universal. o3-mini scored
$74$ of $100$ as a planner on the same data, and the authors describe the results as
mixed across models. Their work is the published starting point for our PlanBench
section, where we repeat the rescue result and add the control it was missing.

Two further results bear on how such pipelines are graded. Planetarium
\citep{zuo2025planetarium} checks generated PDDL problems for semantic equivalence with
the intended task. Zero-shot, $96.1\%$ of GPT-4o's problem files parse and $94.4\%$ are
solvable, yet only $24.8\%$ are correct, so solvability alone is a weak check on a
generated model. LLMFP \citep{hao2025llmfp} formalizes planning problems as optimization
problems for an SMT solver instead of PDDL. On Blocksworld and Mystery Blocksworld ($602$
queries each) it reaches optimal rates of $96.2\%$ and $77.7\%$ with GPT-4o, where direct
planning with the same model reaches $41.5\%$ and $0.8\%$.

Agentic systems wrap the solver in a loop. In the system of \citet{lamalfa2026endtoend},
an orchestrator LLM writes the PDDL domain and problem, agents refine them, and the
validated files are passed to an external planner. With GPT-5-mini it raises the average
PlanBench success rate from $76.6\%$ to $91.6\%$ over three runs of $50$ problems per
task, with GPT-5-mini as the judge of success. \citet{gobel2026agentic} expose a step-wise
PDDL simulator to Claude Haiku~4.5 over MCP and let the model choose one action at a time
until a plan validator accepts the plan. On $102$ IPC Blocksworld instances this agentic
setup solves $66.7\%$ against $63.7\%$ for direct planning, at $5.7$ times the token cost
per solution. Their tool set contains no planner, so the model does the search itself.

\subsection{Tool Use and the Decision to Call}
Equipping LLMs with external tools is now standard, from learned tool invocation
\citep{schick2023toolformer} to large API repertoires \citep{qin2024toolllm}. The Model
Context Protocol \citep[MCP;][]{anthropic2024mcp} standardizes how tools are described to
a model and how its calls are relayed, and it is how we expose planners and validators.
BFCL checks a model's calls against the expected function and arguments with an
abstract-syntax-tree method \citep{bfcl2025}, and $\tau$-bench measures multi-turn
tool-agent-user interaction \citep{yao2024taubench}. Benchmarks built on live MCP servers
extend this to many tools and long tasks. They score runs with execution-based evaluators
\citep{luo2025mcpuniverse} or with rule-based checks and an LLM judge
\citep{wang2026mcpbench, guo2025mcpagentbench}, and MCPAgentBench adds distractor tools to
test selection \citep{liu2025mcpagentbench}.

Fewer studies ask whether the model decides to call at all. BFCL separates items where a
call is expected from items where none is \citep{bfcl2025}. MetaTool
\citep{huang2024metatool} asks the model directly whether a query needs a tool and finds
that most models lack this awareness. When2Call \citep{ross2025when2call} poses a four-way
choice that includes a tool call, and builds its items so that answering directly is
always a hallucination. SMART \citep{qian2025smart} studies the opposite error, calling
tools for questions the model could answer from its own knowledge. In each of these the
right decision is fixed when the item is built. In our tasks a sound tool is always
relevant, but the model can often answer correctly without it. Whether it calls the tool
is a behavior we observe, and its cost is measured in the final answer.

A parallel line raises tool use through scaffolding, interleaving reasoning with tool
actions \citep{yao2023react} or offloading numeric subproblems to executable programs
\citep{chen2023pot}. HAL \citep{kapoor2026hal} evaluates agents across models and
scaffolds and reports accuracy against dollar and token cost. Our cost-of-pass analysis
follows that practice. Our setting is at the simple end of this range. Each task has one
relevant tool, and that tool is sound, so the closest analog is single-call function
selection.

\subsection{Positioning}
The planner and verifier studies above, the frontier comparison of
\citet{correa2025frontier} among them, measure what a model does alone. The formalizer
pipelines and the system of \citet{lamalfa2026endtoend} measure what a model achieves
together with a solver, and the call to the solver is part of the pipeline.
\citet{gobel2026agentic} give the model simulator tools but no planner, and a run ends
when the validator accepts a plan. In each of these designs, either the model works
without tools or the call is built into the system, so how often a model calls a sound
tool that it is free to skip is not measured. The tool-calling benchmarks do measure that
decision, on items where the right choice is fixed in advance, and they score the
decision itself.

Besides comparing the same model with and without the tools, this paper measures two
quantities on PDDL tasks with sound planning tools. The first is
how often a model calls the tool, under a plain prompt and when one added
sentence asks for it. The second is how often a correct tool result reaches the model's
final answer, which a deterministic checker grades. We know of no earlier study that
reports the two separately for planning tasks. The closest is a preliminary study by \citet{benyamin2025copilot} of an
earlier version of the tool suite we use, which folded both into one success rate
(see the last subsection of this section). Our main tasks hand the model complete PDDL
files, so they do not test whether the model can write PDDL. We test that step on
PlanBench \citep{valmeekam2023planbench}, where the prompts are plain English and the
model must write the PDDL before it can call a planner. The PlanBench section
re-measures the no-tools baseline against the published figures and compares the tools
with a control that keeps the same prompt and removes only the tools.

\subsection{Relation to the Preliminary Study}
\label{sec:prelim}

\citet{benyamin2025copilot} introduced the Planning Copilot, which exposes planners, a
plan validator and a plan simulator to an LLM over MCP. They tested three open-weight
models (Qwen3 at 0.6B and 4B, and GPT-OSS-20B) with and without these tools on the five
task types studied here. With tools, the success rate rose in $14$ of the $15$
model-task cells. On \emph{solve} it went from $0.04$ to $0.78$ for Qwen3-4B and from
$0.28$ to $0.78$ for GPT-OSS-20B, and no model simulated a plan correctly without tools.
The evaluation was preliminary. It used $250$ single-task queries in all, reported the
better of each model's thinking and non-thinking runs, gave no confidence intervals, and
had no control for memorized domains. Its tool-arm system prompt told the model that
calling tools was its only way to solve a task, and a tool-arm trial counted as a success
only if the model had called the correct tool and its final answer was right. Calling and
delivery were therefore folded into one number. The present paper grades $273{,}600$
open-weight trials over two corpora, with confidence intervals and an anonymized-domain
control, and adds a pre-registered study. It confirms the main direction. Against an unaided

baseline with a corrected system message, tools raise the rate of correct delivered
plans by $49.3$ and $38.3$ points for two of the three open models of the pre-registered
study and by $9.3$ points, with an interval that reaches zero, for the third. Frontier
models go from $22$--$29\%$ unaided to $95\%$ with the planner. Unaided simulation is again
close to zero ($0$ of $3{,}000$ answers in the exact format under the shared output
budget), but with the budget and format relaxed, models from 4B up get $22$ to $40\%$ of
trajectories right. The paper changes the reading in two places. First, a model may skip a
tool that it is offered. In the main sweep Gemma-MoE-26B called the plan validator in
$21\%$ of \emph{validate\_plan} trials, against $94\%$ when one sentence in the user turn
asked it to. In the pre-registered study, having the tool available did not lower its
delivered accuracy ($89.2\%$ against $88.3\%$ unaided; difference $+0.9$ points, 95\% CI
$[-1.2, 2.9]$). Second, a correct tool result does not always reach the final answer. In
the same study, on \emph{simulate}, the trajectory returned by the tool left the model no
room to answer in $42.7$ to $53.0\%$ of tool-arm trials, and for no open model does the
tool arm show a gain over the unaided arm on that task.

\section{Methodology}
The main study crosses five PDDL tasks, five open-weight models, two reasoning
modes and three experimental arms, and two frontier models are run on part of
that design. Every response is graded against a deterministic oracle built from
the planners and the validator. This section describes the pipeline, the tools,
the tasks and test data, the models, the arms and the statistics.

\subsection{Experimental Pipeline}
A single evaluation harness turns the test files into graded trials, and every
(model, reasoning mode, arm) cell runs the same four stages. In \emph{setup}, the
harness loads the PDDL domain corpus and connects to the symbolic tools over the
Model Context Protocol \citep{anthropic2024mcp}. In the \emph{oracle} stage, before
any model is queried, an oracle calls those tools directly on every fixture to
produce ground truth: a reference plan, a validity verdict or a state trajectory,
according to the task. A startup check re-confirms each fixture against its
committed label and aborts the run on any drift. During \emph{trials}, for each
task, paraphrase and instance, the harness builds the prompt and serves the model
with vLLM at temperature~0. In the tool arms the model may issue up to ten tool
calls before it commits a final answer. The harness logs that answer with its
token counts and any tool calls. Finally, \emph{grading} scores each response
against the oracle (Table~\ref{tab:tasks}), tags it by failure mode, and
aggregates the scores into success rates with confidence intervals (Metrics).

\subsection{The Tool Suite}
The tools the model may call are sound symbolic planners and validators. Each is
exposed as a Model Context Protocol \citep{anthropic2024mcp} function with a typed
argument schema. The model reads a task description, selects a tool, emits a
structured call and receives the tool's result. Every tool trial, open-weight or
frontier, exposes the same seven tools from two plugins. The solver plugin provides a classical planner (Fast Downward
\citep{helmert2006fastdownward}), a numeric planner (ENHSP \citep{scala2016enhsp}) and
a tool that saves a plan to a file. Each planner call has a wall-clock limit of
$120$ seconds. The validator plugin provides
\texttt{validate\_domain}, \texttt{validate\_problem} and \texttt{validate\_plan}, which
return a VALID or INVALID verdict, and \texttt{get\_state\_transition}, which returns
the state trajectory a plan induces. The four validator tools run \texttt{pyval} (the
\texttt{pddl-pyvalidator} package), a Python plan validator built on the
\texttt{unified-planning} library, and both planners are also called through
\texttt{unified-planning}. Each task has one matching tool. On \emph{solve} there are
two, and the model picks the planner that fits the domain. The harness never narrows
the list, so every tool trial also tests whether the model picks the right tool, and
calls to the wrong tool do occur. The validator tools take an optional
\texttt{verbose} flag, which the harness removes from the schema the model sees and
fixes to false. The tools accept PDDL either as text or as a file path. The harness
gives no paths, so the model has to copy the PDDL text from its prompt into the call.
In the canonical corpus every call that carried a domain argument passed that text.
The tools are deterministic, and what the model contributes is choosing the matching
tool and passing the task's files into it intact. The
suite packages the Planning Copilot tools of \citet{benyamin2025copilot}, whose
evaluation covered the same five task types with $250$ single-task queries in
total ($10$ problems, $5$ prompt variants, $5$ request types). The corpus graded
here runs $4{,}560$ trials in every model-mode-arm cell, about $18$ times that
entire suite per cell. The
verbatim per-task prompts that expose these tools, together with the one-sentence
steering directive, appear in Listing~\ref{lst:prompts}.

\subsection{Tasks, Oracle, and Fixtures}
% TODO(Omer): quotes the earlier Claude runs (Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.6), now in the supplement; the new run is in Table~claude
Each model is given five PDDL tasks, and each task has one relevant tool: \emph{solve}
(find a plan reaching the goal), \emph{validate\_domain} and
\emph{validate\_problem} (judge whether a domain or problem file is well formed),
\emph{validate\_plan} (judge whether a given plan is correct), and
\emph{simulate} (produce the full state trajectory a plan induces, action by
action). Before any model is run, an oracle makes the answer key by calling the same tools on
the original files. On the three verdict tasks the key is \texttt{pyval}'s verdict. On
\emph{simulate} it is the trajectory that \texttt{get\_state\_transition} returns for
the planner's plan. \emph{Solve} has no key plan, and a returned plan counts if
\texttt{pyval} accepts it for the original domain and problem. On the verdict tasks
and \emph{simulate} the checker is the tool, so the tool-verified outcome (defined
under Metrics) is correct by construction whenever the model calls the matching tool
on the unchanged task files. On every task a trial with no call fails that grade by
construction. The tool-verified grade therefore measures whether the model calls the right tool
with intact arguments, and it does not measure the tools' own accuracy. On
\emph{solve} the tool and the checker are different programs. Fast Downward or ENHSP
finds the plan and \texttt{pyval} checks it, although both read the PDDL through the
parser of \texttt{unified-planning}. On the other four tasks the tool and the answer
key are the same program, so a fault in \texttt{pyval} would go unseen on the tool
side and would count against an unaided answer that disagreed with it. We therefore
checked the plan side of the answer key with an outside validator, VAL
\citep{howey2004val}. VAL agrees with \texttt{pyval} on $1{,}731$ of $1{,}732$ plans
($99.94\%$, Wilson 95\% interval $[99.67, 99.99]$): all $1{,}000$ \emph{validate\_plan}
test plans and $731$ of the $732$ distinct \emph{solve} plans graded in the delivered
rerun. In the one disagreement \texttt{pyval} accepts \texttt{pick-up} for the action
\texttt{pick\_up}. A second check covers the $745$ \emph{solve} plans of the two Claude
models that both validators could read. VAL agrees on $734$ ($98.52\%$, $[97.38,
99.17]$), and on all $219$ plans of the tool arm. All $11$ disagreements are one fault
of \texttt{pyval}. When an unaided answer writes an upper-case name exactly as the test
file declares it, \texttt{pyval} rejects the plan, although PDDL names are
case-insensitive. This fault lowers a few unaided \emph{solve} grades. It also fails
some tool calls in which the model rewrote a plan with the file's capitals before
passing it to \texttt{validate\_plan} or \texttt{get\_state\_transition}. We report the
grades as stored and give the VAL grades beside them, labelled post hoc. VAL does not
confirm the rest of the key. On the domain and problem files it agrees with the labels
on only $184$ of $238$ ($77.3\%$). Every miss is an invalid file that VAL accepts,
because VAL ignores text after the \texttt{define} form and reads a missing goal as an
empty one. Those labels rest on a strict reading of the PDDL grammar. The
\emph{simulate} trajectories were not checked with VAL.
Grading is strict (Table~\ref{tab:tasks}). A \emph{solve} answer counts
only if \texttt{pyval} accepts the returned plan. A \emph{simulate} answer counts
only if its entire trajectory equals the oracle's under a canonical normal form
(sorted, case-folded, with the boolean and numeric fluent shapes unified), with no
partial credit and no free-text fallback. Both arms are graded on the model's own
final message, by the same parser. A tool-arm trial is also scored a second time,
from the result that the tool returned on the model's arguments, which shows
whether the model selected the right tool and passed the task's files intact. The
Metrics subsection defines both scores and fixes the delivered answer as the
primary outcome.

\subsection{The Three-Arm Design}
For the open-weight models, every (model, mode, task) is run in three primary
% TODO(Omer): points at material moved to the supplement (paper/supplement_source.tex)
arms, and a fourth, control arm is described below. The frontier models are run in
the first two. Arms are scored separately and never pooled, and every comparison is
between two arms of the same cell:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{no-tools}: the model answers from parametric knowledge. The PDDL
tools are removed and the answer format is requested in the system prompt alone: a
JSON answer on \emph{solve} and \emph{simulate}, a closing verdict line on the three
validation tasks. Per-task JSON-schema-constrained decoding was intended for this arm
but did not take effect, so no decoder-level constraint was active and an unaided
failure can be a failure of format as well as of content (Limitations).
\item \textbf{tool-available (plain)}: the same user prompt, byte for byte, with
the tools exposed. The system prompt mirrors the no-tools one. The two share
their first sentence and their sentence count and differ in the policy clause,
``use the available tool'' against ``use your own reasoning,'' and the no-tools
one adds a reminder of the output format on the verdict tasks. We return to this
difference in Limitations. The difference between this arm and no-tools is the
\emph{availability gap}.
\item \textbf{tool-steered}: the plain prompt plus exactly one appended
sentence that directs the model to use the tool. The difference between this arm
and the plain arm is the \emph{steering gap}.
\end{itemize}
The Results refer to the second and third arms as the plain prompt and the
steered prompt.

The two contrasts correspond to two notions of the function-calling literature,
in the sense of the Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard \citep{bfcl2025}. The

tool when the tool is offered and the user message does not name it. The steered
arm measures \emph{function selection}: whether a model that is told to use a tool
picks the correct one. Each tool's schema names its required arguments, but
nothing forces a model to supply them, and a call that leaves one out returns an
error. The steered contrast therefore covers selection together with argument

\subsection{Metrics, Confidence Intervals, and Signed Significance}
\paragraph{Primary outcome.} The primary outcome on every task and in every arm
is the \emph{delivered} success rate. The answer in the model's own final message
is graded against the deterministic oracle, by the same parser in both arms. This
is what a user of the system receives. A correct result that the model obtained
from a tool and did not carry into its answer does not count. An empty final
message that follows a correct tool result is graded as a failure for the same
reason.

\paragraph{The measures of the three questions.} RQ1 compares the delivered
answers of the no-tools arm and the tool arms on four measures: the false
positives and false negatives of plan verdicts, the share of correct states
against trace length, the share of problems solved against hardness, and plan
length. RQ2 reads the stored tool calls and sorts each with-tools trial by the set
of tools it called. RQ3 takes the trials in which the right tool returned the
right result and reports the share whose delivered answer is wrong. The Results
define each measure where it is used.

\paragraph{Tool-verified success.} In the tool arms we grade each trial a second
time. \emph{Tool-verified} success asks whether a correct result appeared in some
tool response during the trial, whatever the final message then said. The two
grades separate whether the model drove the tool to a correct result from whether
that result reached the answer. The difference between the tool-verified rate and
the delivered rate is the \emph{delivery gap}, and RQ3 measures the same loss
trial by trial. A tool-verified rate is never a headline in this paper, and
wherever one appears it is labeled. We also record \emph{tool\_selected}, whether
the model called the task-matching tool at all, which gives the \emph{calling}
rate. Failures are typed (tool not selected, wrong tool, verdict mismatch,
unparseable output, trajectory mismatch), and responses cut at the output cap are
tagged as truncations and are not counted as content failures.

% TODO(Omer): quotes the earlier Claude runs (Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.6), now in the supplement; the new run is in Table~claude
\paragraph{Storage and what it permits.} Tool calls and their results are stored
in full in every corpus, so calling rates and tool-verified rates are exact
everywhere. The final message is not always stored in full. The main sweep kept
its first $500$ characters, so its tool arms give no delivered rate. The
delivered rates of the tool arms come from the pre-registered rerun and from the
frontier runs, which stored answers up to $65{,}536$ and $16{,}384$ characters
(RQ1).
Where a stored answer ends before it can be graded, the
trial is \emph{indeterminate}: it can be graded neither correct nor incorrect.
Indeterminate trials remain in the frontier \emph{simulate} cells and in the
\emph{simulate} cells of the steering control. A cell that contains them is
reported as a censoring bound, written \cbnd{a}{b}: the delivered rate lies
between ``every indeterminate trial failed'' ($a$) and ``every indeterminate trial
succeeded'' ($b$). Wilson intervals are written $[a,\,b]$, and the two notations
are never mixed in one cell. A claim that involves a bound is asserted only if it
holds at both ends of the bound.

\paragraph{Intervals.} The RQ sections report 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals
that resample whole domains.
Arms
are never pooled: each statistic comes from exactly one (model, mode, arm) corpus,
and only differences between such statistics are formed.

\section{Results}
This section answers three research questions in turn. The analyses that
predate them, which cover the main sweep task by task together with cost, reasoning
% TODO(Omer): points at material moved to the supplement (paper/supplement_source.tex)
mode and contamination, follow in the Supporting Analyses. The questions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{RQ1.}] Do PDDL tools help an LLM do planning-related tasks? We compare
the same model with and without the tools on four measures: the false positives and
false negatives of its plan verdicts (\emph{validate\_plan}); the share of simulated
states that are correct, as a function of the length of the simulation
(\emph{simulate}); the share of problems solved, as a function of how hard the problem
is (\emph{solve}); and the length of the plans it delivers (\emph{solve}).
\item[\textbf{RQ2.}] Does the LLM know to choose the right PDDL tool? Every tool is
offered, and we report the share of trials that call exactly the right tool and no
other, the share that call the right tool at all, and the share that call other tools
as well.
\item[\textbf{RQ3.}] Does the LLM know how to use the tool's output? We report the
share of trials in which the tool returned the right result and the model's final
answer was still wrong.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{A pilot with a larger context window.} A refused or clipped final turn
suggests that more room would recover the tool's result. A pilot checks this on a part
of the data. We reran the \emph{solve} and \emph{simulate} tool trials of the first
problem of every domain with a 32K and a 64K context window, and compare them with the
same trials of the rerun at 16K (Table~\ref{tab:rq3-pilot}). On \emph{simulate} the
share of empty final answers falls from $42$--$43\%$ at 16K to $6$--$11\%$ at 64K. The
share of correct tool results that are lost falls much less: from $92.5\%$ to $83.8\%$
for Gemma-MoE-26B, from $72.9\%$ to $69.0\%$ for Qwen3.5-9B, and from $64.5\%$ to
$49.1\%$ for Qwen3.6-35B. On \emph{solve} the loss falls from
$9.2\%$ to $5.0\%$, from $19.2\%$ to $16.7\%$ and from $20.2\%$ to $11.1\%$ for the same
three models. Room in the context window therefore accounts for the empty answers and
for a part of the loss. Two properties of the task contribute to the rest. The
first is layout. Our grader reads a single JSON value, so a trace written as one JSON
object per line fails it whatever its content. Reading such traces as well lowers the
loss of Gemma-MoE-26B at 64K from $83.8\%$ to $72.4\%$ and that of Qwen3.6-35B from
$49.1\%$ to $46.4\%$, and leaves Qwen3.5-9B at $69.0\%$. The second is
conversion. The state-transition tool returns each state as a map from every predicate
to true or false, in a functional notation, while the answer must list only the true
predicates. Delivering a trace is therefore a conversion of the tool's result and not a
copy of it, and the models make errors in it even on traces of five states. The loss
that RQ3 reports for \emph{simulate} should be read with both properties in mind. The pilot is
small ($114$ to $120$ trials per cell), its three window sizes were run as separate
jobs weeks apart, and the intervals of the three sizes overlap in every cell.

All the results so far come from one test suite, and in that suite the PDDL files are
handed to the model inside the prompt. We wanted to know whether the same pattern
appears on a benchmark that someone else built, so we repeated a smaller version of the
experiment on PlanBench \citep{valmeekam2023planbench}. We use its plan-generation task
in two domains. The first is standard Blocksworld. The second is Mystery Blocksworld,
which renames every predicate and action to an unrelated word and leaves the underlying
problem untouched. The instance files sit in two directories of $501$ and $101$ files,
and the first file in each serves only as the worked example, which leaves $500$ and
$100$ instances to ask. We checked all $602$ files and confirmed that the
renaming is a straight symbol swap with the objects unchanged, so the two domains ask
the same planning question in different words. Every cell uses the same $600$ instances,
and the model is Claude Haiku 4.5 throughout.

One difference from our own suite matters more than the change of domain. PlanBench
prompts are written in plain English, so a model that wants to use a planner has to
write the PDDL itself first. Our suite hands the files over and never tests that step.
We also grade differently here. A trial counts as correct only when the plan in the
model's final answer is valid, so a correct tool call that the model never copies into
its answer is scored as a failure.

We report two results. The main one uses no tools at all and asks where a current small
model stands on the field's own benchmark next to the published GPT-4 figures. The
second compares tools against no tools, and we treat it as a secondary finding. That
tools can rescue planning on the renamed domain is already published
\citep{huang2025limit}. What we add is the control that earlier work did not report: a
no-tools arm that keeps exactly the same prompt and removes only the tools. We also add
a third arm that keeps the tool instruction but attaches no tools, paired significance
tests with confidence intervals, a measurement of how faithfully the model writes the
PDDL, and the measured dollar cost.

\subsection{Adding the Tools}
In the with-tools arm the model can call the same planners and validators used in the
rest of the paper, over the same protocol. Its prompt carries a short system message
that tells it to write PDDL first and gives the answer format. The control arm uses that
same system message word for word and simply has no tools attached. Because the wording
is identical on both sides, any difference between them comes from the tools rather than
from what the model was told.

Table~\ref{tab:pb-wt} gives the four cells. On Mystery the gap is as wide as the
benchmark allows, $71.8\%$ with tools against $0.0\%$ without. On standard Blocksworld,
where the control arm already reaches $47.8\%$, tools still lift the cell to $68.3\%$,
an increase of $20.5$ points. Both
comparisons are paired on the same instances and tested with an exact McNemar test. On
Blocksworld, $202$ instances were solved only with tools and $79$ only without, giving
$p=1.4\mathrm{e}{-}13$. On Mystery the split is $431$ to $0$, giving
$p=3.6\mathrm{e}{-}130$.

The Blocksworld with-tools figure is the cautious reading. The run was interrupted and
$18$ instances were asked a second time. On their first attempt all $18$ had used up
their tool-calling turns and returned nothing, so we count them as failures. Reading
them the other way would raise that cell by $1.4$ points.

Once the tools are available the renamed domain is not harder than the standard one.

Mystery's favor, with $119$ instances solved only on Blocksworld and $140$ only on

gap also depends on a notation loss in the Mystery cell. With the corrected extractor

\subsection{What the Tools Fix}
The natural explanation for the Mystery collapse is that renaming the vocabulary breaks
the model's ability to search for a plan. Our with-tools data point somewhere else. For
every trial we recovered the PDDL the model sent to the planner and compared it against
a correct reconstruction of the same instance, using a parser we deliberately kept out
of the model's own tool set. The model's PDDL captures the intended meaning on $96.3\%$
[$94.5$, $97.6$] of standard trials and $97.8\%$ [$96.3$, $98.7$] of Mystery trials, so the
renaming does not damage it.
Turning an English description into PDDL is a copying job that does not depend on what
the words mean, and unfamiliar names leave it intact. Every with-tools trial in both
domains called the planner.

The same measurement shows what still holds the standard cell back. When the model's domain
file carries the intended meaning, the planner returns a solvable problem $99.5\%$ of
the time. When it does not, that falls to $1.6\%$, and none of the $35$ mismatched
trials produced a correct plan. The limit is how faithfully the model writes the domain,
and the reason is visible in the English source. The standard Blocksworld description
leaves out part of the physics, never saying that moving a block leaves the one beneath
it clear, and the model writes down what the text says. The Mystery text is a mechanical
rendering of the same rules, which is why $99.5\%$ of Mystery domains come out right and
the renamed cell scores a little higher than the standard one.

This adds a step to the failure sequence we track for our own tasks. When the prompt
already contains PDDL, a with-tools trial can fail in three ways: the model never calls
the tool, it calls with bad arguments, or it fails to report what came back. PlanBench
puts an earlier step in front of those, which is writing the problem down in the
tool's language. We treat \textsc{formalize} as the first stage of the PlanBench
sequence (Figure~\ref{fig:funnel}, top right, draws all four stages), and it belongs to
the benchmark, because it cannot arise anywhere the PDDL is supplied. Once a plan clears that stage and the extractor, the delegated search is right
$95.9\%$ of the time on Blocksworld and $97.3\%$ on Mystery.

\subsection{What Could Distort These Numbers}
\label{sec:pb-distort}
Several measurement problems affect these cells. The figures we report as primary
correct for none of them, as preregistered. For the two that come from the plan
extractor we add a post hoc regrade at the end of this section. Most
work against the tools arm. On Mystery, $125$ of $569$ delivered plans were thrown out
because the model wrote its actions in a PDDL shorthand the benchmark's extractor does
not read. Trials that used
up their tool-calling turns without answering are counted as failures, which costs the
standard and Mystery with-tools cells $10.5\%$ and $5.2\%$ of their trials. Failures to
write usable PDDL are charged to the model as well.

One problem works the other way, and it affects the cell we report as $0.0\%$. In $479$
of the $600$ Mystery no-tools trials, the model wrote out its reasoning in a way that
leaked extra actions into the plan the extractor picked up, which spoils plans that
would otherwise have been graded. Reading only the text inside the model's own answer
block gives $26$ of $600$, or $4.3\%$ [$3.0$, $6.3$] (coincidentally equal to the
published GPT-4 figure), and we report both figures. The
leak pushed the no-tools cell down, so this problem worked against the published $0.0\%$
instead of inflating our result. The comparison with the tools arm survives either
reading. Under the stricter one, $412$ instances are solved only with tools and $7$ only
without, giving $p=6.4\mathrm{e}{-}112$. Two further measurements
agree that the collapse is real. The $121$ trials with no leak score $0$ of $121$, and
the published prompt, which carries no system message and shows no leak, scores $0.7\%$.

After the readout we wrote a corrected extractor and regraded all four cells with it.
It reads only the model's final answer block, and it accepts the PDDL shorthand as well
as the English phrasing. Every plan it extracts is checked by the same validator
against the same instance files. This regrade is post hoc and was not preregistered,
so the figures above remain the primary result. The corrected figures are $47.8\%$
[$43.9$, $51.8$] without tools and $70.2\%$ [$66.4$, $73.7$] with tools on Blocksworld,
and $4.3\%$ [$3.0$, $6.3$] and $94.3\%$ [$92.2$, $95.9$] on Mystery, where $566$ of
$600$ trials deliver a valid plan. All $125$ shorthand plans are valid, and no trial moved
from correct to incorrect. Both tool contrasts grow, to $22.3$ points on Blocksworld
($207$ instances solved only with tools and $73$ only without,
$p=5.1\mathrm{e}{-}16$) and to $90.0$ points on Mystery ($541$ and $1$,
$p=7.5\mathrm{e}{-}161$). The comparison between the two domains changes more. With
the corrected extractor the with-tools gap is $24.2$ points in Mystery's favor, with
$28$ instances solved only on Blocksworld and $173$ only on Mystery
($p=1.0\mathrm{e}{-}26$), and the equivalence criterion is not met at either margin.
The notation loss fell almost entirely on the Mystery cell, which is what brought the
two primary figures close together. Our prompt asked for the English phrasing, so a
shorthand answer is still a failure to follow the instruction, and the primary grading
counts it as one.

\subsection{Scope and Cost}
This arm covers one model. We registered a stronger second model as an extension and did
not run it, so we cannot say how the tools gain changes with model strength on this
benchmark, and the comparison with GPT-4 in Table~\ref{tab:pb-nt} rests on a single
model of ours. Measured cost was \$$39.87$ for the $2{,}400$ trials of the main
comparison and \$$2.61$ for the instruction-only arm. The whole PlanBench arm came to
about \$$46$ including the calibration runs, or roughly \$$0.017$ per trial.

\section{Discussion}
% TODO(Omer): quotes the earlier Claude runs (Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.6), now in the supplement; the new run is in Table~claude
\paragraph{What the three answers say together.} A sound tool guarantees its own
result. What a user receives depends on two further steps that the guarantee does not
cover: the model has to call the tool, and the tool's result has to reach the final
answer. RQ2 and RQ3 measure these two steps, and RQ1 measures their combined effect.
When both steps hold, the benefit can be large. With the tools the models deliver a
valid plan in $78$--$95\%$ of the trials. The open-weight models solve $43$--$69\%$
% TODO(Omer): points at material moved to the supplement (paper/supplement_source.tex)
unaided in the control of RQ1, and the tools are clearly ahead for two of those three
models and for both frontier models. If the model
does not call the tool, the tools change little. Gemma-MoE-26B calls the plan
validator in $20.0\%$ of the plain-prompt trials, and its verdict errors with the tools
offered are close to its unaided ones. If the result does not reach the answer, the
tools bring no gain. The same model calls the state-transition tool in $99.7\%$ of its trials, loses
$90.8\%$ of the correct traces it gets back, and ends with no more exact traces than
it produces unaided.

\paragraph{Choosing the tool.} On the validation tasks and on \emph{simulate} the
models rarely pick a wrong tool. The open issue there is whether they call at all, and
one sentence that names the tool closes most of that gap in each of the three
open-weight models on which we ran it.
On \emph{solve} the models call more than the task needs. Much of the addition is a
check of the planner's plan, which costs tokens. Calling the planner that does not fit
the domain is a different matter: a numeric planner
still returns a valid plan for a classical problem, and that plan can be far longer
than the reference. With thinking on, two of the three larger open-weight models call
the right tool less often, so reasoning mode and tool calling are not independent
settings.

% TODO(Omer): quotes the earlier Claude runs (Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.6), now in the supplement; the new run is in Table~claude
\paragraph{Using the output.} A verdict is a single word and almost always survives.
A plan or a state trace has to be restated in the answer, and the loss grows with the
length of what must be restated. In the open-weight runs the largest registered cause
is a final turn that was refused or cut because the conversation, which already held
the tool's result, had filled the context window. More room recovers a part of these results. In a pilot
with 32K and 64K windows the empty answers largely disappear, while the share of correct
traces that our grader counts as lost falls by $4$ to $15$ points and stays between
$49\%$ and $84\%$. Two properties of the \emph{simulate} task contribute to what
remains. The grader accepts one layout of the trace, and some correct traces are written
in another. The tool also returns states in a form that differs from the form the answer
must take, so the model has to convert the result and cannot copy it. On this task the
figure therefore measures layout and conversion as well as the model's use of the tool's
content. The pilot is small and was not registered. On the full rerun, reading the second
layout lowers the lost share by at most $13$ points in any cell, so layout does not
explain the finding.
For the two Claude models, the output-budget probe raised the output allowance

on \emph{simulate} (Supporting Analyses).

% TODO(Omer): quotes the earlier Claude runs (Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.6), now in the supplement; the new run is in Table~claude
\paragraph{Unaided planning.} Our unaided open-weight models solve $43$--$69\%$ of a set of
% TODO(Omer): points at material moved to the supplement (paper/supplement_source.tex)
small problems in the control of RQ1, and the frontier models $22$--$29\%$ under a
different request, with thinking off and a prompt without solved examples. \citet{correa2025frontier}
report that frontier reasoning models, prompted with two solved examples, rival strong
planners on larger and freshly generated tasks. The two studies differ in models,
reasoning mode, prompt and problems, so our unaided figures describe the models and
settings we ran and should not be read as a general limit on what language models can
plan. Our question starts where theirs ends: what changes when the same model may call
a planner.

\paragraph{For practice.} Three observations follow for a system that puts a planner
or a validator behind a tool interface. Offering the tool is not enough for every
model, and a sentence that names it is a cheap repair. A model that calls several
tools on one task may call a planner that does not fit the domain, so the choice of
planner is worth constraining. A tool whose output the model must restate needs room
in the context window for that restatement, and in our pilot room alone did not secure
it.

\section{Limitations}
% TODO(Omer): quotes the earlier Claude runs (Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.6), now in the supplement; the new run is in Table~claude
Several constraints bound these conclusions. The measures of RQ1 and the
classification of RQ2 were defined after the data were collected, so they are
exploratory. Only the loss categories of RQ3 on the rerun were registered. The
comparisons that need the complete final answer cover five models: three open-weight
models with thinking off, and two Claude models on a single prompt wording. The rerun
% TODO(Omer): points at material moved to the supplement (paper/supplement_source.tex)
behind them did not meet its registered parity criterion against the main sweep, so it
is reported as a separate replication and its figures are never pooled with main-sweep
figures. The planner solves every problem of the test set, so the results do not cover
problems that the planner itself cannot solve. Its runtime barely separates the
problems, so hardness is measured by plan length. The reference plans are the
planners' first plans and are not shortest plans, so the plan-length comparison is
against a common baseline and makes no claim about optimality. Claude answers were
stored up to $16{,}384$ characters, so Claude figures on \emph{simulate} are lower
bounds where storage cut the answer.

Under \emph{think}=on, reasoning and answer share one decode budget, so a large
fraction of no-tools trials are truncated. We compare the two modes through the
smaller of their differences (Methodology), but we cannot fully separate the tool's
value from the budget in that mode. Raising the cap is not a clean remedy. In pilot
runs, enlarging the shared decode budget (a larger common allowance, not a cap
reserved for the final answer) degraded adherence to the output format, and more
trials emitted unparseable answers. A larger shared cap therefore trades the
truncation confound for a parsing one. Decoding is deterministic (temperature~0), so
each trial is a single sample and our intervals measure uncertainty over the test
instances, not decoding variance. Robustness to higher temperatures is untested.

% TODO(Omer): quotes the earlier Claude runs (Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.6), now in the supplement; the new run is in Table~claude
The roster is five open-weight models from two families, so the results describe
these models and are not a ranking. The unaided results extend in part to a strong
proprietary model. Claude Sonnet~4.6 also fails most \emph{solve} problems unaided
(with no detectable memorization effect, although its \emph{solve} comparison across
corpora is unresolved). On \emph{simulate} it reaches substantial unaided success
(\cbnd{41.7}{61.3}), in line with the budget-decoupled control on the open-weight
models ($22$--$40\%$), so the zero on that task in the main sweep is an effect of the
% TODO(Omer): points at material moved to the supplement (paper/supplement_source.tex)
apparatus and is not a fixed limit of capability (the supplement). The low
calling rate under the plain prompt was observed only in open-weight models. Both
Claude models call the tool on essentially every trial, and the steered prompt was
not run on them, so whether the one-sentence steering repair transfers to proprietary
models with aggressive tool-use post-training is untested here. The test set of $20$
domains spans classical and numeric planning and does not cover all of PDDL's
features. Temporal, durative and conditional-effect constructs are absent, and the
results may differ on richer formalisms.

Model scale is partly confounded with numerical precision: the two largest models
are served AWQ-INT4 and the smaller three in 16-bit. A comparison of plain-arm calling
rates across models therefore cannot separate a model's own tendency to call from an
effect of quantization on tool calling. We describe calling within each model, through
the $P(\text{call})\times P(\text{correct}\mid\text{call})$ decomposition, which does
not rest on that comparison. The availability gap compares two arms whose system
prompts differ by a one-line policy and format clause as well as by the presence of
the tools. The steering gap, where the plain and steered prompts differ by exactly one
appended sentence, is the cleaner of the two contrasts.

both the tool and its stated availability, and that contradiction biases the test
toward equivalence. Its verdicts cover the six pooled units and the eight eligible

The two figures compared in the matched cell are different measures, the with-tools
effect in the tool-verified rate and the control on the model's own answer, so the
comparison only sets them side by side. The control was run on a later apparatus than
the canonical sweep, beside a neutral-prompt arm in the same job. Its bearing on the
canonical steering gap therefore rests on how closely that neutral arm reproduces the

unmeasured run-to-run variation of deterministic decoding, since no prompt was ever
sampled twice under one apparatus. No \emph{think}=on control exists for
Gemma-MoE-26B, whose \emph{think}=on unaided cell saturates the decode budget under
every apparatus available to us.

part of the task, so a model that reasons correctly and emits malformed output scores
as a failure. We report the decomposition by failure type and the budget-decoupled

(the supplement). What the three

and under one that states the answer format, is measured in a pre-registered control

(RQ1, the supplement; Technical Appendix), and that control supplies

The largest constraint that we imposed on ourselves is storage. The main sweep
stored final answers as $500$-character snapshots, a decision made before the
delivered answer was fixed as the primary outcome. Its tool arms therefore give no
delivered rate, and this paper reports none from them. The pre-registered rerun
stores answers in full for the three $\geq$9B models with thinking off and is what
RQ1 and RQ3 rest on. It did not meet its parity criterion, so its figures do not stand
in for main-sweep cells. The frontier \emph{simulate} cells have been probed with a

larger allowance (Supporting Analyses). Under a $64$K allowance with full storage
they become exact. The budget explanation meets its pre-registered criterion for
Haiku and does not meet it for Sonnet, and the residual failures ($30$--$35\%$ of
tool-arm trials) are wrong or unparseable trajectories and are not truncations. A
larger allowance therefore narrows the gap without closing it.

The serving runtime did not surface per-trial reasoning traces for the model family
used, so \emph{think}=on reasoning is evidenced through completion-token counts and
not through archived chains of thought. Wall-clock latency is not recoverable from a
batched server, so we use token cost, not time, as the measure of efficiency. Finally,
\emph{validate\_domain} carries a $5{:}1$ positive-to-negative imbalance that widens
its negative-arm interval. We therefore report balanced accuracy for that task, which,
like $F_1$, is not inflated by the imbalance that a pooled metric would reward.

\section{Future Work}
Four extensions follow from limits stated in this paper. The first is a test set that
includes problems the planner does not solve within its time limit. On such a problem a
planner call returns a timeout, so the comparison with the unaided model would show what
a model delivers when its tool fails, and up to which problem size the tools still raise
the share of problems solved. The second is reasoning mode. RQ1 and RQ3 are reported
here with thinking off only. Answering them with thinking on needs complete stored
answers and a decode budget reserved for the final answer, because in the main sweep the
reasoning and the answer shared one budget. The third is a registered experiment on the
state traces that RQ3 counts as lost. The pilot enlarged the context window for the
open-weight models, and the output-budget probe enlarged the output allowance for the
Claude models, so neither varied both settings on one model. The experiment would cross
the two settings. The fourth is to ask the three questions on planning formalisms that
our tool suite and test set do not cover, such as temporal planning, for which the suite
has no planner.

A fifth direction goes beyond the three questions. The planners and validators studied
here take a PDDL domain as input, and none of them produces one. Algorithms for learning
action models produce a domain from observed plan executions
\citep{yang2007arms, aineto2019fama, juba2021sam}, and \citet{arora2018review} survey
them. A language model could be given such an algorithm as a tool, and the domain it
delivers could then be compared with the domain that the algorithm learns from the same
executions without a language model.

\section{Conclusion}
% TODO(Omer): quotes the earlier Claude runs (Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.6), now in the supplement; the new run is in Table~claude
We asked three questions about giving a language model sound PDDL tools. The tools
help on most of what we measured. With the tools the models deliver a valid plan in $78$--$95\%$ of the trials. The
% TODO(Omer): points at material moved to the supplement (paper/supplement_source.tex)
open-weight models solve $43$--$69\%$ unaided under a corrected system message, and
the tools stay ahead for two of the three. Verdict
errors on plans fall for four of five models. The benefit
shrinks as problems grow, and state traces degrade with length with or without the
tools. The models usually choose the right tool. One model, however, seldom calls the
plan validator until a sentence tells it to, and on plan generation the models add
calls, some of them to a planner that does not fit the domain. The last step,
delivering the tool's result, fails most often. A correct verdict almost always reaches the
answer, and a correct plan is lost in $5$--$13\%$ of the trials. A correct state trace
is lost in most of the trials by the open-weight models and in at least a third by the
Claude models. At our context window the largest registered cause is a final turn with
no room left, but a pilot with a larger window recovers only part of the loss. A planner
or a validator behind a tool interface therefore helps a language model most when the
model is told to call it, calls the one that fits the task, and has a short result to
restate.

\section{Technical Appendix}

\paragraph{Canonical trajectory normalization (\emph{simulate}).}
A \emph{simulate} answer is graded by deep equality against the oracle trajectory
after a canonical normal form is applied to both, so the open-weight roster's $0\%$
unaided rate in the main sweep is not an artifact of predicate notation in the grader.
Per state, the boolean predicate list is case-folded, whitespace-collapsed and sorted.
Predicate atoms are compared in a notation-neutral form, so an s-expression rendering
\texttt{(on a b)} and a functional rendering \texttt{on(a,b)} match. Numeric fluents
are keyed by their canonical parenthesised form. Case, internal spacing, notation
style and ordering therefore never affect the verdict. Only the set of true
predicates and the fluent values do. For example, an oracle
state whose true predicates are \texttt{(clear c)} and \texttt{(on a b)} is matched by
a model state listing \texttt{(ON A  b)} then \texttt{(clear c)}, and a numeric fluent
\texttt{(fuel t1)} of value \texttt{5} matches \texttt{5.0}. A single missing or
extra predicate in any state fails the entire trajectory.
